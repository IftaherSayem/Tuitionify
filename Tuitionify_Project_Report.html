ABSTRACT

Private tuition is a deeply embedded part of secondary and higher-secondary education in Bangladesh, yet finding a trustworthy tutor still depends on word of mouth, printed leaflets, and scattered Facebook groups. This report presents Tuitionify, a full-stack web application that brings the process online for the International Islamic University Chittagong (IIUC) community. Tuitionify connects university-student tutors with guardians and students through a structured hiring loop — posting, filtering, applying, accepting, and reviewing — while keeping personal contact details private until a relationship is established.

The system is built as a React single-page application on the frontend, a Node.js and Express REST API on the backend, MongoDB Atlas for persistence, and Firebase for authentication. It is shaped around the Bangladeshi context in concrete ways: salaries are quoted in BDT and bounded to a realistic range; the class system, curriculum subjects and Chittagong areas are enforced as validated option lists; and mobile numbers are normalised and validated as real Bangladeshi numbers, because hiring ultimately ends in a phone call.

Because trust is the product, the design treats safety as a core requirement rather than an afterthought. Reviews may only be written by seekers who have actually engaged a tutor; reviews and reports require a confirmed email address; restricted accounts are refused everywhere, including on public pages; and contact details are removed from API responses server-side rather than merely hidden in the interface. A security review of the completed system identified ten findings, of which eight were resolved, one was patched at the dependency level, and one was assessed and deliberately not changed after inspection showed it was not reachable. A Vitest suite of seventy-two tests covers the rules that matter most, and was itself verified by deliberately removing protections and confirming the tests fail.

Keywords: tuition marketplace, trust and safety, review integrity, privacy-gated contact sharing, Bangladeshi education system, MERN, Firebase, MongoDB, Express, React, Vitest

CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION

1.1 Project Overview

Tuitionify is a web-based tuition marketplace that connects tutors with guardians and students. It is scoped to the IIUC community, where the two sides of the market naturally coexist: university students who want part-time teaching income, and families in and around Chittagong who want a qualified tutor for a school-going child.

The application supports two user roles. A tutor builds a public profile stating subjects, class levels, preferred areas, expected salary and qualifications, then browses and applies to posted tuitions. A seeker — a guardian or a student — posts a tuition requirement, reviews the tutors who apply, and accepts one. A third role, admin, exists for moderation: verifying tutors, handling reports, and restricting accounts that misbehave.

What distinguishes the system from a simple listings board is that it models the hiring relationship, not just the advertisement. An application has a state. Accepting an applicant closes the tuition. Contact details become visible only once one party has agreed to share them. A review can only be written by someone the tutor actually agreed to work with. These rules are enforced on the server, where a user cannot bypass them.

1.2 Problem Statement

The existing process for arranging private tuition in Bangladesh suffers from several concrete problems.

Discovery is inefficient. Guardians rely on personal referrals, leaflets stapled to notice boards, and Facebook groups where posts scroll out of view within hours. A guardian in one part of Chittagong has no reliable way to find a suitable tutor in the same area.

There is no verifiable reputation. A tutor's claim about their own experience cannot be checked. Existing platforms that do show ratings rarely restrict who may rate, so a rating can be inflated by friends or by throwaway accounts. A rating that anyone can write is not evidence of anything.

Contact information is exposed prematurely. When a phone number is published openly, it is harvested and misused. Guardians are particularly reluctant to publish a number attached to a post that also mentions a child and a home area.

Generic platforms do not fit the local context. International tutoring sites assume different currencies, grade systems, and subject names. A guardian looking for a Class 9 Higher Math tutor in Chawkbazar is not served by a platform built around a different curriculum.

1.3 Objectives

The project set out to achieve the following.

  1. To design and implement a functioning two-sided marketplace covering the complete hiring loop: post, discover, apply, accept, and review.
  2. To keep personal contact details private by default, and to share them only after a mutual step has taken place.
  3. To ensure that ratings reflect real engagements, by permitting reviews only from seekers who have demonstrably engaged the tutor.
  4. To fit the Bangladeshi education context precisely, through validated subject, class-level and area lists, BDT salary bounds, and Bangladeshi mobile number handling.
  5. To provide moderation capabilities so that misuse can be reported and acted upon.
  6. To subject the finished system to a security review, resolve what is resolvable, and state the remaining limitations honestly.
  7. To verify the rules that carry the most risk with an automated test suite.

1.4 Scope

Within scope. Role-based registration and authentication; tutor profiles with photo upload; tuition posting with structured requirements; filtered search over both tutors and tuitions; the application lifecycle including withdrawal; a contact-request mechanism; engagement-gated reviews with tutor replies; bookmarking of both tuitions and tutors; reporting and admin moderation; an admin analytics dashboard; and a light and dark theme.

Outside scope. In-app messaging, online payment or salary escrow, video lessons, scheduling and calendar management, a mobile application, and identity verification against national documents. Chapter 9 discusses which of these are the natural next steps.

CHAPTER 2 — BACKGROUND AND LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 The Private Tuition Market in Bangladesh

Private tuition occupies an unusual position in Bangladeshi education. It is not a marginal supplement but a widely assumed part of preparation for public examinations, particularly at the SSC and HSC stages. Households across a broad income range treat tuition costs as a routine expense, and university students form a large and willing supply of tutors, for whom teaching is one of the few flexible sources of income compatible with a class timetable.

The market is therefore large, active, and almost entirely informal. Arrangements are made verbally, terms are agreed without documentation, and reputation travels by word of mouth within a neighbourhood or a circle of relatives. This informality is efficient within a small social network and fails completely outside it. A guardian who has recently moved to a new area has no network to draw on.

Three features of the local context bear directly on the design of any software intended to serve this market. First, the currency and salary expectations are specific: monthly rates quoted in BDT, typically in the low thousands, and a system that accepts an unbounded number invites both typos and unrealistic postings. Second, the academic structure is specific: classes numbered through the school system, then HSC, then admission preparation, with subject names following the national curriculum. Third, and most practically, the transaction concludes by telephone. A platform may mediate discovery, but the hiring conversation happens on a mobile call, which makes a valid mobile number a functional requirement rather than an optional profile field.

2.2 Existing Solutions and Their Gaps

Several categories of existing solution were examined before design began.

Social media groups. Facebook groups dedicated to tuition in particular cities are the dominant tool in practice. Their advantage is reach and zero setup cost. Their weaknesses are structural: posts are unstructured free text, so filtering by class, subject or area is impossible; content is ordered by recency and social engagement rather than relevance; there is no notion of an application, so a post attracts a disorganised flood of comments; and phone numbers are posted publicly, exposing both parties.

Local classifieds platforms. General-purpose classified sites carry tuition listings. They provide structure for search but treat tuition as a commodity listing. There is no concept of a tutor profile with a verifiable history, no application state, and no reputation mechanism tied to actual engagements.

International tutoring marketplaces. Established global platforms solve the reputation and scheduling problems well, and some implement contact gating and payment escrow. They are, however, built around different curricula, currencies and payment instruments. Their subject taxonomies and grade systems do not map onto the Bangladeshi system, and their payment rails are largely unavailable locally.

A recurring weakness across all three categories deserves emphasis, because it motivated one of the central design decisions in this project. Where ratings exist at all, they are rarely tied to a completed transaction. A platform that allows any registered account to rate any tutor produces a rating that measures social reach rather than teaching quality. Since a rating is precisely the signal a guardian most wants to rely on, an unverified rating is worse than no rating: it invites misplaced confidence.

2.3 Key Concepts Applied

Two-sided marketplace design. The system must serve two distinct user groups whose value to each other depends on the presence of the other side. This motivates giving both sides a symmetric discovery path: seekers browse tutors, and tutors browse tuitions, so neither side must wait passively.

Progressive disclosure of personal data. Rather than treating contact information as either public or private, the system treats it as conditionally visible. The condition is a mutual step: an accepted application, or an explicitly approved contact request. This principle is applied server-side, so the data never reaches a client that is not entitled to it.

Verified reputation. Reviews are gated on evidence of a real engagement. The design requirement is that the gate must depend on an action taken by the party being reviewed, so that the reviewed party cannot be targeted by a reviewer acting alone.

Defence in depth. Authorisation is checked at multiple layers rather than at a single gate: token verification, account loading, restriction checks, role checks, resource ownership checks, and field-level whitelisting on write operations. Chapter 5 details how these compose.

Principle of least privilege on writes. Every write endpoint accepts an explicit whitelist of fields rather than assigning a request body wholesale, so that sensitive fields such as verification status cannot be set by a client.

The project followed an incremental and iterative process. A minimum working system was built first — authentication, the two roles, tuition posting, and applications — and further capability was layered on in successive increments, each ending in a runnable state.

This suited the project for two reasons. The requirements were not fully known at the outset, since several rules only became apparent once the system could actually be used; and the trust rules in particular emerged from reasoning about how a working marketplace could be abused, which is difficult to do productively in the abstract.

Two choices warrant fuller justification.

Delegating authentication to Firebase. Implementing password storage, reset flows and email verification correctly is a significant undertaking with a high penalty for error. Delegating it removes an entire class of vulnerability: since the application never receives a password, there is no credential hash in the database to be leaked. The cost is a dependency on an external provider and the need to verify tokens on every request.

MongoDB rather than a relational database. The data is naturally document-shaped, particularly the user record, where a single collection serves two roles with different populated fields. The relationships are shallow — no query requires deep joins. The trade-off is that referential integrity is not enforced by the database, so cascade deletion is handled explicitly in application code, as described in Chapter 5.

The unique compound indexes are load-bearing rather than incidental. 4.4 Trust and Safety Rules

These rules are the design response to the weaknesses identified in Chapter 2. Each is enforced in the API.

4.4.1 Review Eligibility

A seeker may review a tutor only if at least one of the following holds:

  1. the tutor was accepted on a tuition that the seeker themselves posted, or
  2. the tutor approved a contact request from that seeker.

The design constraint is that both conditions require an affirmative act by the tutor. A seeker acting alone cannot manufacture either one, and therefore cannot manufacture standing to review.

The reason this rule matters more than its size suggests is that average rating is the primary sort key of the public tutor directory. Registration is free and unlimited, so without the rule a person could create accounts and move a competitor down the ranking or their own profile up. The unique index prevents one account from reviewing twice; it offers no protection against ten accounts each reviewing once.

// server/utils/engagement.js
const myTuitionIds = await Tuition.find({ createdBy: seekerId }).distinct('_id');

if (myTuitionIds.length) {
  const accepted = await Application.exists({
    tutor: tutorId,
    tuition: { $in: myTuitionIds },   // scoped to the seeker's own postings
    status: 'accepted',
  });
  if (accepted) return true;
}

return Boolean(await ContactRequest.exists({
  tutor: tutorId, seeker: seekerId, status: 'approved',
}));

4.4.2 Confirmed Email for Reviews and Reports

Reviews and reports additionally require a confirmed email address. This is what makes the previous rule expensive to circumvent at scale, since each additional account now costs a real, reachable mailbox. Posting a tuition and applying are deliberately not gated this way, as the added friction on the core marketplace loop would outweigh the benefit; both are already restricted by role and by rate limit.

4.4.3 A Valid Mobile Number Before Applying

Because hiring concludes by telephone, a tutor must have a valid Bangladeshi mobile number saved before applying. A guardian who accepts a tutor they cannot telephone has not hired anyone, and the application merely clutters their list.

Numbers are validated as 01 followed by an operator digit in the range 3–9 and eight further digits, and are normalised on write, since the same number is written many ways in practice.

The profile field itself remains optional, so a tutor browsing the site is not forced through a form prematurely. The requirement is applied at the point where it becomes meaningful, and the dashboard displays a notice beforehand so that it is not discovered only at the moment of applying.

4.4.4 Restriction Applies Everywhere

A restricted account is refused on authenticated routes. On the public tutor and tuition detail pages, which accept optional authentication in order to show additional data to signed-in users, a restricted account is treated as an anonymous visitor. Without this, a restricted seeker would retain the contact visibility granted by an earlier approved request, and the restriction would be only partially effective.

4.4.5 Server-Side Removal of Personal Data

Every response path that could carry a telephone number or email address removes the field before responding, rather than relying on the interface to conceal it: tutor listings, tutor detail, tuition detail, applicant lists before acceptance, and a seeker's own contact-request list before approval. A user reading the raw API response therefore sees exactly what a user reading the rendered page sees.

5.1.1 State Management

State is held in two React contexts, with no external state library. AuthContext owns the Firebase user, the database profile, and the derived role flags. ThemeContext owns the light or dark preference, persisted to local storage. All other state is local to a component. At this scale the approach keeps data flow readable; a larger application would justify a dedicated store.

5.1.2 Token Handling

A single Axios interceptor attaches the authentication token to every outbound request, so no individual call site can forget it. The token is requested fresh each time rather than cached, which allows the Firebase SDK to refresh it transparently as it approaches expiry.

// client/src/api/client.js
api.interceptors.request.use(async (config) => {
  const user = auth.currentUser;
  if (user) {
    const token = await user.getIdToken();
    config.headers.Authorization = `Bearer ${token}`;
  }
  return config;
});

5.2.3 Proxied Image Upload

Profile photographs are uploaded through the API rather than directly from the browser, because the image host requires an API key. Any value exposed to the client build is embedded in the public JavaScript bundle and can be extracted from it, so the key is held only on the server. The endpoint also re-checks the decoded size and distinguishes an invalid key from a rejected file from an upstream rate limit, so that a misconfiguration is not reported to the user as a bad photograph.

5.3 Security Hardening Applied

5.3.1 Query Input Coercion

Express parses bracket notation in a query string into an object, so a request such as ?area[$ne]=x would introduce a database operator where a value was expected. Every filter value is therefore coerced to a scalar before use.

// server/utils/sanitize.js
export function asString(value) {
  if (value === undefined || value === null) return '';
  if (Array.isArray(value)) return typeof value[0] === 'string' ? value[0] : '';
  return typeof value === 'string' ? value : '';
}

Figure 5.4: Forcing a query parameter to a scalar

Search terms are additionally escaped and length-capped, which prevents both a server fault from an unbalanced bracket and a denial-of-service through a pathological expression.

5.3.2 Write Whitelisting

No endpoint assigns a request body wholesale. Each copies only an explicit list of permitted fields, so that verification status, restriction status, rating aggregates, posting status and ownership cannot be set by a client.

5.3.3 Validation at the Persistence Layer

CHAPTER 7 — SECURITY ANALYSIS

7.1 Security Posture

The security review was conducted against the completed system, with the aim of finding what a motivated user could do that the design did not intend. The review covered the API routes, the middleware, the models, and the client. Its overall conclusion is that the system holds together through defence in depth: the interface hides what a user should not act on, the API refuses what a user may not do, and the schema refuses what should never be stored.

The principal strengths identified were as follows.

  • No password is stored. Authentication is delegated to Firebase, so there is no credential hash in the database to leak.
  • Mass assignment is closed. Every write copies from an explicit whitelist, so verification status, restriction status and ratings cannot be set by a crafted request.
  • Identity is taken from the signed token. The email address is read from the verified token and is not editable, which is what keeps administrator identification meaningful.
  • Query injection is systematically prevented. Every filter value is coerced to a scalar, and search expressions are escaped and capped.
  • Ownership is re-checked on every mutating route.
  • Personal data is removed server-side rather than hidden in the interface.
  • The image-host key never reaches the client.
  • Errors do not leak internals. Database errors are translated to generic responses, and server-fault text is suppressed in production.
  • Cross-site request forgery is structurally absent. Authentication travels in a header rather than a cookie, so a third-party page cannot cause an authenticated request to be made on the user's behalf.

7.2 Findings and Their Resolution

Ten findings were identified. Of these, eight were resolved by code changes, one was patched at the dependency level, and one was assessed and deliberately not changed after inspection. Two of the findings deserve a fuller account, because the honest description of both is that initial severity assessments changed on closer inspection.

Finding 5, the administrative report filter. The query-string status value was passed directly into a database filter, so a crafted value could inject a comparison operator. This was first assessed as a high-severity injection. On inspection it sits behind an administrator guard, and an administrator can already list every report by omitting the filter entirely; there is no privilege boundary to cross. It was corrected for consistency with the sanitisation discipline used throughout the codebase, not because it was exploitable.

Finding 4, the dependency advisory. The audit reported a denial-of-service issue in a transitive dependency of the Firebase Admin SDK. The vulnerable code path only executes when a buffer argument is supplied to a function that the SDK never calls. The dependency was updated to a patched version where available; the remainder of the advisory chain requires a breaking major-version upgrade of the SDK for no security gain, and is knowingly left in place. This is stated openly so that the decision can be revisited.

7.3 Accepted Limitations

Three limitations are known and deliberately accepted. Each is recorded here so that the limits of the system's assurances are explicit.

7.3.1 A restriction does not survive a new registration

The restriction flag is attached to a database record, not to a person. A restricted user can register a new account with a different address and return. This cannot be closed in application code, because the application has no identifier that follows the person across registrations; the address must also be disabled in the identity provider. This is the one gap the review could not resolve, and it is stated rather than hidden.

7.3.2 Rate limits are per-instance

The rate-limiter counters are held in process memory. On a serverless platform, each instance counts independently, so the limits are per-instance rather than global. This stops a single client from hammering an endpoint, but a determined attacker distributing requests across many instances would see separate counters. A shared store such as Redis would be required for strict global limits, which is disproportionate for the current scale.

7.3.3 Administrative actions are not audited

Restricting an account or dismissing a report leaves no record of who acted or when. With a single administrator this is immaterial; with several it becomes a gap in accountability. An audit log is the first item on the future-work list in Chapter 9.

7.4 Assessment of the Highest-Value Fix

The most consequential finding was not a classic vulnerability. It was the absence of any requirement that a review reflect an actual engagement. No attacker tooling is needed to exploit that absence; free registration alone is sufficient, and the asset under attack is the ranking signal the entire directory is ordered by. This is the kind of weakness that matters most in a marketplace, and it is the finding that most justifies the review having taken place. It is now closed, enforced at the API, and protected by the test suite.

8.3 Discussion

What worked. The two-sided design proved itself in practice: symmetric discovery meant both roles had a reason to return, and the structured hiring loop gave every interaction a state that the interface could show. The trust rules, added late and after the features worked, improved the system more than any feature had, which validates the decision to review the completed system rather than declare it finished.

What the design traded. Delegating authentication to Firebase removes a class of vulnerability but adds a single point of dependence; the system is only as available as its identity provider. Document storage avoided join complexity but made cascade deletion a matter of application discipline. Per-instance rate limiting is a pragmatic choice whose ceiling is known. These are recorded so that the trade-offs are deliberate rather than accidental.

What was learned. The most transferable lesson is that a marketplace's trust rules must be enforced where they cannot be bypassed, and that the natural place is the layer between the client and the database. A rule enforced only in the interface is a request; a rule enforced in the API is a guarantee; a rule enforced in the schema is a certainty. The project uses all three layers, in that order of increasing authority.

CHAPTER 9 — CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

9.1 Conclusion

This project set out to build a tuition marketplace that fit the Bangladeshi education context and did not inherit the trust problems of existing solutions. The delivered system meets that aim.

Tuitionify is a complete, working marketplace: two user roles with symmetric discovery, a structured hiring loop from posting to an earned review, privacy-gated contact sharing, and an administrative surface for moderation. It is shaped around its context in enforceable ways — validated curriculum lists, BDT salary bounds, and Bangladeshi mobile numbers — rather than in cosmetic ways. Its trust rules are enforced at the API and protected by a test suite whose capability was demonstrated rather than assumed.

The security review found and resolved eight issues, patched one dependency, and assessed the remaining two honestly, including the one gap — restriction not surviving re-registration — that application code alone cannot close. The most valuable single change to come out of the review was the requirement that a review be earned.

For a course project, the significance of this work lies less in any individual feature than in the discipline with which the whole was assembled: requirements traced to implementation, rules enforced at the correct layer, tests checked for the ability to fail, and limitations stated rather than omitted.
